\begin{abstract}
Batch reviews of B2B accounts receivable often miss intraday credit deterioration because payment behaviour, financial fundamentals, and litigation signals are evaluated in disconnected, periodic cycles. This paper presents ACIS-X (Autonomous Credit Intelligence System, Extended), an event-driven multi-agent framework on Apache Kafka that continuously maintains a live per-customer risk score. Specialised agents coordinate exclusively through Kafka events across five core processing layers, an autonomous model governance plane, and a cross-cutting self-healing plane. The pipeline features native Kafka Exactly-Once Semantics (EOS), three-tier signal fusion, SHAP audit persistence, statistical drift tracking via Population Stability Index (PSI), online challenger model benchmarking, and heartbeat-driven recovery from committed offsets. To evaluate the architecture under diverse macroeconomic stress cases, we utilize a jump-diffusion stress test harness. The evaluation demonstrates real-time streaming throughput, horizontal consumer group scalability, autonomous fault recovery with storm prevention, and the effectiveness of multi-signal fusion over single-factor heuristics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Management of enterprise credit risks calls for constant evaluation of payment status, financial position, and potential litigation. Daily or weekly batch cycles delay action when risk rises within a day. Event streaming platforms such as Apache Kafka [1] and emerging multi-agent designs [2,11] make it practical to maintain a live credit view instead of an overnight refresh. However, while recent deliberative LLM-based multi-agent systems [2,12] offer qualitative reasoning,their multi-second latency and non-deterministic outputs make them unsuitable for high-throughput, sub-50ms financial streaming pipelines subject to strict operational risk controls. This work introduces ACIS-X (Autonomous Credit Intelligence System, Extended), a Kafka-based reactive, event-driven multi-agent framework (ED-MAS) grounded in the Actor model for real-time B2B credit intelligence. It targets three gaps in prior practice: (1) latency of batch monitoring; (2) siloed behavioural, financial, and litigation signals; and (3) fragile pipelines without autonomous recovery and drift governance. The contributions are: a layered Kafka-only reactive agent architecture with a cross-cutting self-healing plane and autonomous model governance; end-toend Kafka Exactly-Once Semantics (EOS); a three-tier fusion path combining behavioural metrics, external financials, and litigation into one score in [0,1] backed by regulatory policy guardrails; an autonomous governance agent supporting model-risk governance principles aligned with SR 11-7 via PSI drift tracking and challenger model benchmarking; heartbeat monitoring with deterministic restart from committed offsets; and a comprehensive test suite measuring latency, deduplication, scaling, predictive quality, weight sensitivity, and fault recovery under an LFTD-style jump-diffusion stress harness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Traditional credit scoring methods like Z-Score proposed by Altman [3] and more recently explainable ML methods for corporate credit [4,5] all involve offline generated or periodically updated feature tables, thus making them less suited to intraday monitoring although SHAP [6] makes these approaches more interpretable. Ensemble or hybrid approaches provide better prediction results [5], yet they remain batch learning methods and do not process invoice stream in real time. Event sourcing and processing pipelines based on Kafka can be used for financial fraud analytics with sub-second latency [7,8], although they focus mainly on transaction-level scoring, not on B2B credit profiling which combines financial fundamentals with litigation data.

The multi-agent approach to credit scoring is gaining attention too. For instance, MASCA [2], Kubam’s agentic credit scoring system [11] and CreditAgent [12] study the use of LLM reasoning and MARL for credit evaluation. Nevertheless, these solutions are focused more on credit scoring than on event stream processing. Recent stream processing microservice solutions incorporate circuit breakers and replay mechanism [10], yet usually restore processing after failover by restarting entire containers, not offering more granular per agent failover through heartbeat-based failure detection.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

ACIS-X maintains a live risk score for each customer $c_i$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}(c_i,t) = \mathcal{F}\bigl(\mathcal{B}(c_i,t),\;\mathcal{E}(c_i,t),\;\mathcal{L}(c_i,t)\bigr) \in [0,1],
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is behavioural history, $\mathcal{E}$ external financial indicators, and $\mathcal{L}$ litigation exposure. Design targets are sub-second processing, usable scores under partial signals, zero silent loss of committed work across restart, and idempotent duplicate handling.

The runtime architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:arch}. The layers 1--4 sit on top of a common Apache Kafka event bus; layers 5 (persistence), 6 (model governance), and the system plane reside below. Agents only communicate via Kafka: Layer~1 deals with customers, invoices, payments, and behavioral indicators; Layer~2 uses financial APIs and litigation information to enrich data and aggregate signals; Layer~3 calculates payment risk and updates the compound score; Layer~4 performs credit policy and collections action; Layer~5 keeps track of system state; Layer~6 measures drift and assesses challenger models; the system plane manages time ticks, monitoring, self-healing, DLQ monitoring, and runtime placement.

\noindent\textit{Architectural Rationale: Kafka-Native Actor Model vs. External Stream Engines.} A central design decision in ACIS-X is using a unified Kafka event log rather than introducing external distributed stream-compute clusters (e.g., Apache Flink). While dedicated streaming engines provide distributed state backends, they also introduce additional operational complexity, synchronization overhead between clusters, and higher total cost of ownership (TCO). ACIS-X avoids these requirements by using Kafka partition keying: all business events are strictly keyed by \ texttt {customer\_id}, ensuring that events for the same customer are routed to the same partition and processed in temporal order. This supports low-contention, per-customer in-memory state tracking within individual agent instances without requiring distributed join coordinators. Local state is periodically persisted to SQLite or PostgreSQL, while committed Kafka offsets enable deterministic recovery after system failures.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Three-Tier Signal Fusion}

\textbf{Tier 1 -- Behavioural signals.} The customer state agent rebuilds metrics from invoices and payments under a per-customer lock. Let $\mathcal{I}(c_i)$ be customer $c_i$'s invoice set, $I_k$ an invoice, and $\mathcal{P}$ the payment set. With payment/due timestamps $\mathrm{paid}_k, \mathrm{due}_k$, on-time ratio (OTR) and average delay are:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{OTR}(c_i) &= \frac{|\{I_k : \mathrm{paid}(I_k)\leq\mathrm{due}(I_k)\}|}{|\mathcal{I}(c_i)|}, \\
\mathrm{AvgDelay}(c_i) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{k}\max\bigl((\mathrm{paid}_k - \mathrm{due}_k),\,0\bigr).
\end{split}
\label{eq:behavioural}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Tier 2 -- External signals.} The external data agent caches financial ratios for 90 days, mapping them to financial risk $\mathcal{E}(c_i)\in[0,1]$. Litigation scraping scores court exposure over 365 days as $\mathcal{L}(c_i)\in[0,1]$.

\textbf{Tier 3 -- Fusion.} The aggregator agent fuses external signals as:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{ext}}(c_i) = w_{\text{fin}}\cdot\mathcal{E}(c_i) + w_{\text{lit}}\cdot\mathcal{L}(c_i)\in[0,1],
\label{eq:aggregation}
\end{equation}
with baseline weights $w_{\text{fin}}=0.60$ and $w_{\text{lit}}=0.40$, treating fundamentals as the primary baseline and litigation as a severe but sparser supplement. (We evaluate sensitivity to these weights in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Payment Risk Prediction and Regulatory Guardrails}

The payment prediction agent scores each open invoice. Feature vector $X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ encompasses normalised delay, credit utilisation, invoice ratio, late rate, external/litigation terms, and rating penalty. A Random Forest ($M=50$ trees) yields base probability $r_{ml}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(X)$:
\begin{equation}
r_{final}=\max\bigl(0,\min(1,r_{ml}+r_{policy})\bigr).
\label{eq:prediction}
\end{equation}
Here $r_{policy}$ represents a deterministic regulatory guardrail: rating penalties ($+0.10$ for B, $+0.20$ for C) plus litigation adjustments ($+0.15$ if $\mathcal{L}>0.1$, $+0.25$ if $\mathcal{L}>0.5$). In enterprise credit governance, rare but catastrophic tail risks (e.g., active insolvency litigation) must not be smoothed out by statistical ensembles. Decoupling statistical $r_{ml}$ from explicit policy constraints $r_{policy}$ ensures that configured credit-risk policies are deterministically enforced.

For auditability, we persist exact SHAP attributions~\cite{lundberg2020local} computed in polynomial time via TreeExplainer: $f(X)=E[f(X)]+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_j$. Policy additives sit outside this decomposition and are logged alongside the SHAP vector for complete mathematical transparency.

\subsection{Risk Refinement and Self-Healing Protocol}

The risk scoring agent forms the adjusted risk $r_{\text{adj}}$ from prediction $r_{\text{pred}}$:
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{adj}}=\max\Bigl(0,\min\bigl(1,r_{\text{pred}}+0.85\cdot\Delta_{\text{behavioural}}+0.30\cdot\Delta_{\text{temporal}}\bigr)\Bigr),
\label{eq:refinement}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_{\text{behavioural}} = 0.40a_{\text{overdue}}+0.20a_{\text{payment}}+0.15a_{\text{delay}}+0.15a_{\text{outstanding}}+0.10a_{\text{exposure}}$ ranks operational urgency across normalised factors $a_{\cdot}\in[0,1]$. Temporal velocity $\Delta_{\text{temporal}}\in[-0.2,0.3]$ captures trend direction.

\noindent\textit{Self-Healing Protocol.} Agents emit heartbeats every $T_H=10$\,s. Monitoring detects a timeout after 60\,s of silence (6 missed beats), triggering an event-driven restart request to resume consumer execution from the last committed offset.

\subsection{Autonomous Model Governance and Challenger Benchmarking}

To support model-risk governance principles aligned with SR~11-7, Layer~6 introduces the \texttt{ModelGovernanceAgent}. It tracks streaming predictions across sliding windows ($W=200$) and computes the Population Stability Index (PSI):
\begin{equation}
\text{PSI} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} (P_b - Q_b) \cdot \ln\left(\frac{P_b}{Q_b}\right),
\label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
where $P_b$ and $Q_b$ are baseline and current empirical quantile proportions across $B=10$ bins. Values $\text{PSI} \ge 0.10$ trigger automated drift warnings. In parallel, an online challenger model (linear SGD classifier) is continuously trained and benchmarked against the champion Random Forest, publishing periodic audit reports to \texttt{acis.system}.

\subsection{Jump-Diffusion Synthetic Stress Test Harness}

To evaluate ACIS-X across diverse macroeconomic stress cases without exposing confidential commercial ledgers, we implement a jump-diffusion synthetic stress test harness:
\begin{equation}
dZ_t = \kappa (\mu - Z_t) dt + \sigma_Z dW_t + J_t dN_t,
\label{eq:jumpdiff}
\end{equation}
where $Z_t \in [0,1]$ is a latent enterprise stress factor, $\kappa$ the mean-reversion rate, $\sigma_Z$ diffusion volatility, and $J_t dN_t$ a Poisson jump process representing liquidity shocks. The harness conditions customer delinquency on $Z_t$ via fat-tailed Pareto delay mixtures across Calm, Stressed, and Crisis regimes. This jump-diffusion harness is used to generate diverse and reproducible stress scenarios for architectural and comparative evaluation, not to establish production-level statistical fidelity.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The solution is implemented in Python~3.11 across $\sim$55 files within seven packages: agents, governance, runtime, self-healing, monitoring, registry, and schemas. The entry point creates Kafka topics in advance and launches agents as separate OS processes managed by supervisor, with a maximum of 5 attempts in 2 minutes. Messages use a Pydantic-validated schema (v1.1) containing the correlation ID; validation failures are routed to a dead letter queue.

\noindent\textit{EOS for Native Kafka}. Transport-level de-duplication uses Kafka idempotent producers, all-in-sync-replica acknowledgments, and bounded in-flight requests. Consumers use committed read isolation and consume only committed transaction records. When transactional processing mode is enabled, Kafka transactions ensure atomicity for consume-transform-produce operations through transactional offset commits, including across topic boundaries. Downstream in-memory LRU filtering and database uniqueness constraints on the primary key provide additional defense-in-depth protection against consumer reconnection or replay. This design preserves reliable message delivery and strengthens recovery guarantees while keeping processing semantics consistent across failures, retries, and replays.

Table~\ref{tab:ml} illustrates an essential architectural distinction: conventional supervised models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, GBDT) achieve high metrics (AUC up to 0.997) because they are trained with full supervision directly on the synthetic generator's mathematical labelling function. In contrast, ACIS-X operates as an online, label-free streaming framework that requires no historical default training labels. In this label-free setting, ACIS-X multi-signal refinement yields a modest mean F1 improvement over the naive heuristic rule ($0.758\pm0.016$ vs $0.730\pm0.034$, winning in 4/5 seed trials, paired $t=1.42, p=0.23$) while critically providing continuous probability ranking and temporal velocity (AUC $0.862\pm0.025$) that single-threshold heuristics cannot generate. Supervised models confirm that external features provide strong discriminative signal when explicit default labels are available.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) shows that behavioral refinement is the key factor behind improved ranks ($\Delta\rho=0.060$, $p<0.001$), while SHAP explainability adds less than 1,ms per event. The fault injection study (Table~\ref{tab:fault}) demonstrates sub-millisecond self-healing decision latency under single-node conditions without restart storms. This metric measures decision latency and restart dispatch latency; in production distributed environments, full recovery latency will also depend on consumer group rebalance timeout (3 to 10 seconds). The governance plane further shows that real-time PSI and KS tests can run online alongside event-based pipelines.


\noindent\textit{Limitations and Domain Context.} We explicitly acknowledge four core limitations: (1)~\textit{Synthetic vs. real data}: synthetic stochastic generation models stylized delinquency dynamics but cannot fully replicate non-linear macroeconomic contagions or strategic invoice deferrals; (2)~\textit{Evaluation scope}: results represent architectural and systems validation (latency, scaling, self-healing, idempotency, and fusion) under controlled conditions; (3)~\textit{Production readiness}: deployment requires empirical backtesting on enterprise ERP ledgers and multi-datacenter disaster recovery drills; and (4)~\textit{Sample size of multi-seed trials}: paired significance tests use $n=5$ random seeds; while ranking trends are consistent across seeds, larger seed sets ($n \ge 30$) will provide greater statistical power. Furthermore, the 60-second heartbeat timeout is tailored for B2B trade credit (30--60 day terms), where a 60-second window provides near-instantaneous intraday response compared to 24--168 hour batch cycles, while suppressing failover flapping from transient GC pauses.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

ACIS-X is a Kafka reactive multi-agent credit pipeline featuring three-tier fusion, native EOS transport guarantees, autonomous model governance, SHAP audit persistence, and heartbeat-driven recovery. On the automated test suite, all 180 tests passed; pipeline P95 latency reached 16\,ms and scaling reached $3.49\times$ at 4 workers. Architectural ablation confirmed behavioural refinement delivers the largest rank lift ($\Delta\rho=0.060$) with $<$\,1\,ms SHAP overhead, while Sensitivity analysis showed a predictable increase in ranking correlation with financial-signal weight within the tested synthetic scenarios. Fault injection demonstrated sub-millisecond self-healing decision latency under controlled conditions with 100\% endurance success. Future work includes empirical validation on enterprise ERP ledgers and multi-cluster disaster recovery drills.
